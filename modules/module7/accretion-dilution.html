Accretion / Dilution: does EPS improve, and does that mean value was created?

Accretion/dilution analysis estimates how a transaction changes earnings per share. A deal is accretive when pro forma EPS is above standalone acquirer EPS, and dilutive when pro forma EPS is lower. But accretion is not the same as value creation. Students should interpret EPS effects alongside deal premium, synergies, financing risk, and return on invested capital.

Analyst purpose: Students should compute EPS impact, identify the drivers, and avoid treating accounting accretion as proof that the transaction creates economic value.
How the calculation works

From standalone EPS to pro forma EPS

The accretion/dilution calculation begins with the acquirer’s standalone net income and shares. The model then adds target net income and after-tax synergies, subtracts after-tax interest expense on new debt, subtracts lost after-tax interest income on cash used, and divides by the new pro forma share count.

Standalone EPS = Acquirer Net Income / Acquirer Shares After-Tax Synergies = Pretax Synergies × (1 - Tax Rate) After-Tax Interest Expense = New Debt × Cost of Debt × (1 - Tax Rate) Lost After-Tax Interest Income = Cash Used × Cash Yield × (1 - Tax Rate) New Shares Issued = Stock Funding / Acquirer Share Price Pro Forma EPS = Pro Forma Net Income / Pro Forma Shares Accretion / Dilution % = (Pro Forma EPS / Standalone EPS) - 1

A transaction can be accretive because the acquirer uses cheap debt, issues little stock, or buys a high-earnings target. That does not automatically mean the acquisition price is justified. EPS impact is one diagnostic, not the final answer.

Student checkpoint: Is EPS accretion coming from real operating improvement or from financing mechanics?
